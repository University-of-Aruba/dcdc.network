★ Deadline 31 August 2026 Headline call

Remote access to AI and supercomputing, open to all Small Island Developing States

UNESCO and the Computing and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory at the Brazilian Center for Physics Research (AI-Lab/CBPF) have opened a joint call for remote access to artificial intelligence and high-performance computing infrastructure. The LAB-IA facility pairs HPC, machine learning, and scientific instrumentation for research across astrophysics, geophysics, materials science, quantum technologies, and biophysics. The call sits under UNESCO's Remote Access to Lab Equipment initiative and its Recommendation on Open Science.

Every one of the six Dutch Caribbean islands is a Small Island Developing State, which means the whole network is eligible. The call is open to MSc and PhD students, postdoctoral researchers, and junior faculty from SIDS, as well as from Latin America and the Caribbean, Africa, and Ukraine. You need a clearly defined research project and strong English. Projects start in October.

Feature

What the first twenty survey responses tell us

By Esther Plomp, DCDC Project Lead

Twenty people have now filled out the network survey. Seventeen are on Aruba, two on Curaçao, and one answered from Belgium. Nothing has come in yet from Bonaire, St. Martin, Saba, or Sint Eustatius, so what follows is a picture of the network's Aruban core rather than of the network. That is the honest caveat, and it is also the clearest reason to fill the survey out if you have not: the islands that answer are the islands the programme gets built around.

Even so, the patterns in these twenty responses are clear enough to act on. English is the language every respondent is comfortable in, followed by Dutch (18), Papiamento/u (16), Spanish (7), and French (2). We will keep English as the primary working language and extend into Dutch and Papiamento/u where we can.

Members come from a wider range of places than we expected. Research and education accounts for eleven respondents and government or public service for seven, with the rest working in healthcare, the private sector, as independent researchers and consultants, or across several sectors at once. That range matters, because the network is meant to reach the wider research community and not only the universities.

Asked about the main difficulties they face in their work with data, nineteen of the twenty named something. Only one respondent reported no major issues. Two problems tie at the top: not enough time or staff, and data that does not move between organisations. Concerns about sharing data follow, then unclear rules and policies, and the plain difficulty of finding data in the first place.

The open answers go further. Reaching a usable sample size in a small island population. Storing and publishing datasets without risking a leak. Public trust, and how rarely the purpose of collecting data gets explained. Data quality and interpretation. The absence of a shared vocabulary. Working without a team. Lacking the tools to run the analysis at all.

What would make sharing easier turns out to be less technical than it looks. Respondents point to collaboration and trust between colleagues. People keep their own storage systems, which do not always produce clean data, and they hesitate to hand anything over when ownership is unclear. Organisations do not compile data on any regular footing. Red tape between institutions makes cooperation impossible in practice. Two members named the missing piece directly: a platform that satisfies the relevant legislation and lets institutions exchange data. Another framed it as mindset, arguing that responsibility and accountability have to be settled before sharing can happen at all.

“Data sharing is frustrated by the lack of an accessible platform for data sharing and the lack of an open data culture in research in Aruba.”

We asked what people would change if they had a magic wand. Skills came back loudest: R, data coding and cleaning, visualisation, web design, and a working knowledge of the legislation. Awareness followed, particularly around reuse. Close behind sat the platform again, a Caribbean repository where data is stored structurally, shared easily, in a format someone can actually use, and where the contributors' work is respected. Political permission to share made the list, as did knowing how to make data attractive to decision makers.

One answer stood out. Rather than asking for funding to cover expensive licences, a respondent asked for access to tools that replace them, naming open source options that can be customised, R instead of SPSS. Funding did appear on the wishlist, but to expand the capacity to make data available rather than to keep paying for software.

Respondents also offered to help. They volunteered support with access to software and tools, with training in data management and visualisation, and with sharing data and handling sensitive data. They want to connect to people working on similar problems, to international networks, and across the islands. Advocacy for open data and engagement with the wider public came up repeatedly.

On format, training and workshops win, online or in person, alongside step-by-step guides. Peer-to-peer learning drew interest. One-on-one support drew the least.

Most members want to stay informed and want to attend training and online sessions. That enthusiasm for online formats is changing our thinking, and we are now looking seriously at running some future activities online. The next chances to join in person are the Blue Wave Analytics: Introduction to R workshop in Curaçao in September and the Kick-Off Conference on Aruba in November.

We will build the Kick-Off Conference programme around these responses, which is the plainest reason to answer the survey now. The programme is not fixed yet.

Training

Blue Wave Analytics: Introduction to R comes to Curaçao

Introduction to R for SPSS Users, the two-day course that ran for the first time in Aruba this April, comes to Curaçao this autumn as Blue Wave Analytics: Introduction to R. Same structure and the same objectives: read a CSV, filter and summarise it, plot it, and walk out with a script that still runs tomorrow. What changes is the data. The worked examples run on Curaçao's undefeated CONCACAF qualifying campaign, so participants learn the tools on numbers they already argue about. The football is the vehicle and not the payload: the objectives stay import, dplyr, ggplot, a basic test, and a script that reruns.

It is aimed at researchers, students and analysts moving off SPSS toward reproducible, open tools, and it assumes no prior R. It runs over two days, Wednesday 23 and Friday 25 September, at the University of Curaçao, with a free day in between to let the first day settle. Rendell de Kort, the network's administrator, teaches it, and Marjorie Alfonso, our coordinator in Curaçao, hosts. The two days are one course rather than two, so plan to come to both.

Registration is open now and places are limited. An optional R installation clinic runs before the first day, and we send setup instructions to everyone who signs up.

Network news

The DCDC Network is now a DataCite consortium

In August the network became a DataCite member, as a consortium. That means we can mint persistent identifiers for the research outputs our universities share, which is the practical machinery behind the F in FAIR: an output with a DOI can be found, cited and counted, and one without it usually cannot.

Two things follow for members. Our outputs become visible in the wider research ecosystem rather than only on our own repositories, and we can finally see what the network is producing and what happens to it afterwards. If you have connected your ORCID to DataCite, anything deposited also appears on your ORCID profile without you lifting a finger.
